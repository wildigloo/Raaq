## Differential Diagnosis
The differential diagnosis for hyponatremia can be remembered with the following mnemonic: 
**he said fake sugar fails; kidneys, adrenals, and thyroids dipped in nutrition; cirrhotic hearts waste medications; extrarenal**

1. Pseudohyponatremia
2. Hyperglycemia
3. Advanced renal failure
4. Primary polydipsia
5. Malnutrition
6. Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone release
7. Primary, Secondary, and Tertiary Adrenal Insufficiency
8. Hypothyroidism
9. Congestive Heart Failure
10. Cirrhosis
11. Diuretic and Other Medication Induced Hyponatremia
12. Cerebral salt wasting syndrome
13. Extrarenal salt loss

### Pseudohyponatremia [1]
The serum consits of solid content, which consists of proteins and lipids, and fluid content in which sodium is exclusively confined. Technically, hyponatremia should be defined as the occurrence of decreased sodium concentration in the *fluid content* of serum. Notice that the serum solids and serum water content must add up to 100%. Usually, solids account for about 7% of serum volume. However, some laboratory methods measure the concentration of sodium in the total serum volume instead of exclusively the fluid component of serum. Thus, in patients with hyperproteinemia or hyperlipidemia, conditions that cause increased solid content of fluid, laboratory findings will underestimate the true serum sodium concentraiton: pesudohyponatremia.

### Hyponatremia due to Hyperglycemia and Other Unmeasured Solutes
When blood glucose levels are elevated, water is drawn out of cells into the extracellular space via osmosis. The increased water in the serum dilutes the sodium, reducing the serum sodium concentration, despite not affecting the total serum sodium. However, because the concentration of sodium in the fluid compartment of the serum did actually decrease, this would be called hyponatremia due to hyperglycemia, not pseudohyponatremia from hyperglycemia; in other words, the serum concentration of sodium in the fluid compartment of serum is actually decreased, unlike in pseudohyponatremia where the concentration of sodium in the fluid compartment is the same. Solutes that are very osmotically active like mannitol, contrast dye, and glycine can also draw water out of cells into the serum and cause dilutional hyponatremia just like glucose.

### Hyponatremia due to Advanced Kidney Disease
Because sodium is osmotically active, it draws fluid around it. If sodium enters the urine, water follows it; excessive natriuresis can result in severe hypovolemia. To conserve effective circulating volume, the kidneys will prevent excretion of sodium as best they can. In states of volume overload, say after drinking a lot of water, the kidneys will try to excrete mainly water while minimizing the excretion of urine. The kidneys accomplish this objective by first allowing sodium and water and most other solutes to move from the lumen of the proximal convoluted tubule of the nephron into the glomerular capillaries. Throughout later portions of the nephron, sodium is continually reabsorbed while water mainly remains in the urine unless ADH is released. The kidney is made up of many nephrons. In chronic kidney disease, nephrons become damaged and destroyed, with fewer nephrons being able to excrete water while retaining sodium. The remaining nephrons work harder (hyperfiltration), but, after a point, the compensation is not enough, and retention of water occurs. This water retention causes dilutional hyponatremia.

### Primary Polydipsia
Primary polydipsia is defined by an excessive intake of fluid because of an abnormality in the brain's thirst drive. Examples include psychogenic polydipsia and dipsogenic polydipsia, the later of which may arise from hypothalamic injury. Secondary polydipsia is excessive thirst driven by a compensatory response to hyperosmolarity of the serum; it usually does not manifest with hyponatremia.

### Malnutrition and Beer Potomania
Excessive intake of beer with little intake of other foods can result in hyponatremia due to lack of intake of sodium. This phenomenon is known as beer potomania. Other causes of malnutrition can result in hyponatremia as well, however.

### Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Release [2]
Syndrome of Inappropriate Antiduiretic Hormone Rlease (SIADH) is defined by unsuppressed release of ADH from the pituitary. The differential diagnosis for SIADH includes medications, CNS disturbances, malignancies, surgery, pulmonary disease, hormone deficiency, hormone administration, HIV, hereditary causes, and trauma; these causes can be remembered through the mnemonic: **Medical brains, cant do surgery except on lung homrones and hereditary HIVes; where they cause trauma**

#### Medications 
Medications like carbamezapine, cyclophosphamide, SSRIs, NSAIDs, opioids, and methylenedioxymethamphetamine (MDMA or ecstasy) can increase sensitivity to ADH or increase the release of ADH.

#### CNS Causes
Hypothalamic dysfunction or pituitary dysfunction can both lead to excessive ADH release; common etiologies include stroke, head trauma, brain tumors, and CNS infections. These conditions can result in central diabetes insipidus.

#### Cancers
Various malignancies can cause SIADH. Sometimes, cancers like small cell carcinoma of the lung can produce ectopic antidiuretic hormone. It is important to not confuse SIADH from a medication side effect with SIADH from the cancer itself, as some chemotherapeutics like cyclophosphamide are known to cause SIADH.

#### Surgery
Neurosurgery can induce CNS injury leading to primary SIADH. However, this is not as common as SIADH arising nonneurological surgery secondary to the stress placed on the body: pain and general anesthesia can activate visceral afferents and induce the release of SIADH.

#### Pulmonary Diseases
Besides lung cancers, pneumonia, tuberculosis, and lung abscesses are commonly associated with SIADH.

#### Exogenous Hormones
Exogenous hormones like vasopressin, desmopressin, and oxytocin can bind the V2 receptors to which ADH normally binds, mimicking the action of ADH. Even though ADH is not technically elevated in this condition, the medications mimick SIADH.

#### Hereditary Causes of SIADH
Hereditary causes of SIADH are rare. Sometimes, mutations in genes encoding V2 receptors, like *AVPR2*, can result in constant activation of V2 despite having normal ADH levels.

#### HIV Associated SIADH
Though rare, HIV can directly cause SIADH through HIV-related encephalopathy. More commonly, opportunistic infections of the CNS or lungs are responsible for SIADH in patients with HIV.

### Secondary or Tertiary Adrenal Insufficiency and Addison's Disease
Cortisol and ADH production are increased by the hormone CRH from the hypothalamus. Normally, cortisol acts via  negative feedback on the hypothalamus, preventing excessive cortisol and ADH production; deficiency of cortisol results in increased CRH production which not only stimulates cortisol production, but also stimulates ADH release form the posterior pituitary, resulting in increased water retention, causing hyponatremia. Thus in states of hypocortisolism, such as primary, secondary, and tertiary adrenal insufficiency, hyponatremia can occur, and, because water follows sodium, low blood pressure results. However, it is very important to note a subtle distinction: in Addison disease, or primary adrenal insuffiency, both aldosterone and cortisol are deficient because the adrenal gland is unable to produce them. In secondary or tertiary adrenal insufficiency, decreased ACTH from the anterior pituitary or decreased CRH from the hypothalamus, respectively, leads to decreased production of cortisol only: the adrenal cortex can still produce mineralocorticoids like aldosterone, which acts to maintain circulating volume and prevnet hypovolemia. Thus, in primary adrenal insufficiency one would expect a hypovolemic, hypotonic, hyponatremia due to excessive production of ADH and loss of aldosterone. In secondary or tertiary adrenal insufficiency, however, one would expect a euvolemic, hypotonic, hyponatremia due to excessive ADH; however, aldosterone is able to prevent excessive volume loss so euvolemia occurs instead of hypovolemia - still hypotension may be observed in secondary or tertiary deficiency because cortisol normally increases expression of alpha1 receptors on vasculature, allowing for increased vascular tone to prevent hypotension. Excessive ADH, though, is still produced by the posterior pituitary because of loss of feedback regulation, resulting in dilutional hyponatremia.

### Hypothyroidism and Heart Failure with Reduced Cardiac Output
Hypothyroidism reduces heart rate, leading to lower cardiac output, which leads to reduced renal perfusion; basically, the GFR is reduced. Thus, **pressure natriuresis** of the kidneys is reduced. Additionally, low cardiac output stimulates ADH release to maintain blood pressure. The net result is a dilutional hyponatremia caused by fluid retention. It is important to note that hyponatremia is uncommon in mild hypothyroidism; in severe hypothyroidism it can result in **myxedema**. Overall, hypothyroidism causes a dilutional, hypotonic, euvolemic or hypovolemic, hyponatremia. A point of confusion is the RAAS system. One would expect RAAS to be activated with reduced cardiac output because of reduced renal perfusion; however, in hypothyroidism RAAS is inactive, so there is low renin and aldosterone. Low aldosterone results in increased urine sodium in these patients. Overall, ADH contributes to hypervolemia, but persistent edema and the deactivation of RAAS leads to hypovolemia, so volume status remains variable in these patients. The opposite is true in heart failure: patients have decreased renal perfusion resulting in increased ADH production as well as RAAS activaiton. These patients demonstrate hypervolemia, a compensantory attempt to maintain effective circulating arterial volume. Aldosterone in heart failure patients results in low urine sodium.

### Diuretics and Medication Induced Hyponatremia
Various medications are known to cause hyponatremia. Usually, they will cause either hypovolemic hyponatremia or euvolemic hyponatremia, mimicking SIADH, as mentioned earlier. Here we will only mention the former as the later was mentioned previously. Loop and thiazide diuretics prevent renal reabsorption of sodium and water, resulting in a hypovolemic hypotonic hyponatremia.

### Cerebral Salt Wasting Syndrome
Cerebral salt wasting syndrome is still not completely understood, and it is difficult to distinguish from SIADH at times. It causes hypovolemic, hypotonic, hyponatremia, and it often occurs within several days of a subarachnoid hermorrhage or neurosurgical procedure.

### Extrarenal Salt Loss
There are other ways to lose salt besides through excretion of sodium into the urine. Bleeding, diarrhea, repeated emesis, third spacing (severe pancreatitis, anaphylaxis, bowel obstruction, etc) can all lead to loss of effective intravascular volume causing a hypovolemic, hyponatremia.

## Workup [3]
### 1. Correct for hyperglycemia
When working up hyponatremia, one should start by assessing for severe hyponatremia: serum sodium <125 mEq/L with severe symptoms, such as coma, ataxia, seizures, or respiratory depression. If the hyponatremia is not severe, the next step is to assess for hyperglycemia. Serum sodium can be corrected in the presence of hyperglycemia through the following equations: 

- If glucose < 400 mg/dL: True sodium = measured sodium + 0.016 * (serum glucose - 100)
- If glucose > 400 mg/dL: True sodium = measured sodium + 0.024 * (serum glucose - 100)

### 2. Assess Serum Osmolality
If glucose is not responsible for the hyponatremia, assess the serum osmolality. If th eserum osmolality is normal (280 to 295 mOsm per kg), the patient may have pseudohyponatremia, and the patient should be evaluated for hyperlipidemia and hyperproteinemia. If the osmolality is low (<280 mOsm per kg), the patient has hypotonic hyponatremia. If the osmolality is high (>295 mOsm per kg), the patient has hypertonic hyponatremia due to unmeasured solutes (other than glucose as this has already been evaluted for).

### 3. Assess Volume Status
The real question then becomes the evaluation of hypotonic hyponatremia. Common causes of hypotonic hyponatremia that should be screened for now include thiazide use and advanced kidney disease. Thiazide diuretics should be discontinued; if hyponatremia persists, then assess the patient's volume status. Volume status can be assessed with blood pressure measurement, examination of mucosa for dryness, as well as ultrasonography of the vena cava. Patients can then be categorized as hypovolemic (decreased total body water and sodium), euvolemic (suggesting increased total body water and normal sodium), and hypervolemic (increased total body water and sodium, despite low sodium concentration).

### 4. Assess Urine Sodium
Hypovolemia with increased urine sodium suggests the kidneys cannot properly reabsorb sodium from the urine. Etiologies include diuretic and other medication use, mineralocorticoid deficiency; the later results from decreased ability of aldosterone to retain urine sodium. These etiologies indicate a saline unresponsiveness. Administering saline will just result in eventual loss of sodium after a few days.

Hypovolemia with decreased urine sodium suggests the kidneys can reabsorb sodium from the urine, and the hypovolemia is from extrarenal loss of sodium. Etiologies include bleeding, third spacing, vomiting, diarrhea, and bowel obstruction. 

Hypervolemia should be further assessed with urine sodium. Increased Urine sodium suggests acute kidney injury or chronic kidney disease, while decreased urine sodium (<20 mEq per L) suggests heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome, or hypoalbuminemia.

### 5. Assess Urine Osmolality
Euvolemia usually demonstrates increased urine sodium, and should be further assessed with urine osmolality. Increased urine osmolality suggests SIADH, hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, and medication use. Urine osmolality less than < 100 mOsm per kg, however, suggests primary polydipsia or low solute intake (Beer potomania).
