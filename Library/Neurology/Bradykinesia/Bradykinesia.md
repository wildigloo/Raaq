## Differential Diagnosis
1. Parkinson Disease - see below
2. Dementia with Lewy Bodies - progressive cognitive impairment with visual hallucinations, REM sleep behavior disorder, and Parkinsonism; overlaps with PD (both alpha-synuclein mediated); If cognitive symptoms predate, then the diagnosis is DLB, otherwise its PD - **avoid dopaminergic therapies which can exacerbate congition and behavior**. Use cholinesterase inhibitors instead.
3. Multiple System Atrophy - another alpha-synucleinopathy; presents with a range of findings: parkinsonism, cerebellar ataxia, autonomic failure, urogenital dysfunction, and corticospinal involvement; relative *preservation of congition*; shows initiall good response to levodopa and can mimic PD in early stages. Autonomic dysfunction is virtually always present in MSA; stridor, a positive Babinski sign, and cerebellar ataxia (dysarthria, oculomotor dysfunction, or gait/limb ataxia) are all seen in MSA but not in PD. Poly-mini-myoclonus is seen in MSA and not really seen in PD. Hot cross bun sign seen on imaging.
4. Progressive Supranuclear Palsy - tauopathy; present with walking difficulties, unsteadiness, falls and visual symptoms (dry eyes and blurred vision). Symmetrical parkinsonism involving axial musculature. Does not respond to levodopa. Posture is very different because of axial involvement: upright, sometimes retrocollis; often fall backwards especially when turning. Early on they may have square wave jerking eye movements or slowing of vertical saccades. May present with apathy or, paradoxically, impulsivity. Loss of executive function and verbal fluency are common. Imaging shows hummingbird sign.
5. Corticobasal Syndrome - tauopathy; has several asymmetric limb rigidity or akinesia, mimicking PD. Frequently associated with dystonia and myoclonus and its Parkinsonism is levodopa-resistant. May also involve axial musculature. May have a nonresting tremor. Key distinguishing features from PD include: apraxia, cortical sensory loss, progressive non-fluent aphasia, and alien limb phenomenon. Has a wide variety of cognitive and behavioral changes or impairment. Imaging shows asymmetric pattern of atrophy involving frontal and parietal cortices.
6. Drug-induced Parkinsonism - dopamine receptor antagonists, especially typical antipsychotics like haloperidol and chlorpromazine, but also atypical antipsychotics like risperidone and olanzapine can cause parkinsonism. Antiemetics acting on dopamine receptors like metoclopramide and domperidone also documented. Also calcium channel blockers, antiepileptics, dopamine depletors (tetrabenazine), and SSRIs are associated with drug-induced Parkinsonism. Usually causes a symmetrical parkinsonism with prominent bradykinesia and rigidity; DATSCAN is normal in drug-induced causes while abnormal in PD.
7. Toxins - various toxins can cause parkinsonism: pesticides (rotenone, paraquat, organophosphates), solvents, metals, and carbon monoxide poisoning.
8. Vascular Parkinsonism - typically bilateral, symmetrical affecting lower limbs. Upright giat with broad base, short steps, and normal armswing. Upper limbs usually spared and tremor is uncommon. Increased tone with spasticitiy and rigidity and gegenhalten paratonia. Bed exam is relatively normal compared to problems seen while mobilizing. Levodopa trial is recommended as well as control of vascular risk factors.
9. Normal-pressure Hydrocephalus - presence of a triad of ataxia, incontinence, and cognitive impairment or dementia; characteristic enlarged ventricles on imaging. Majority of the time, NPH is associated with parkinsonism but without rigidity and postural instability. Treatable with CSF shunt.
10. Familial Parkinson Disease
11. Wilson Disease
12. Huntington Disease
13. Spinocerebellar Ataxia - presents with parkinsonism combined with cerebellar ataxia and peripheral neuropathy. Seen in subtypes: SCA2, SCA3, and SCA17, which are trinucleotide repeats inherited in autosomal dominant pattern.
14. Fragile X-associated Tremor/Ataxia Syndrome - cerebellar ataxia and intention tremor cause by CGG trinucleotide repeat expansion of FMR1 gene on X chromosome. Can present with parkinsonism, bradykinesia, postural instability, and rest tremor.

## Diagnosis Parkinson Disease
The diagnosis of *clinically established Parkinson disease* requires the following:
1. Bradykinesia AND (tremor OR rigidity)
2. Absence of exclusion criteria
3. At least 2 supportive criteria

The diagnosis of *clinically probable Parkinson disease* requires the following:
1. Parkinsonism
2. Absence of exclusion criteria
3. Balanced numbers of supportive criteria and red flags

Exclusion criteria include:
1. Cerebellar abnormalities
2. Supranuclear gaze palsy
3. Frontotemporal dementia diagnosis within 5 years of disease
4. Primary progressive aphasia diagnosis within 5 years of disease
5. Parikinsonian features restricted to the lower limbs for more than 3 years
6. Treatment with a dopamine receptor blocker or dopamine depleting agent, consistent with drug induced parkinsonism
7. Absence of a response to high-dose levodopa despite moderate disease severity.
8. Cortical sensory loss 
9. Clear limb ideomotor apraxia 
10. Progressive aphasia
11. Normal DAT scan
12. Diagnosis of alternative condition


## Bradykinesia
Bradykinesia can be elicited using repeated hand movements (finger tapping, or pronation/supination, or foot tapping). Not only a slowness of movement, but also a decrement in the speed or amplitude of movement should be observed. Handwriting should display microgrpahia, in which handwriting becomes progressively small and cramped. It may difficult to observe true bradykinesia when a patient has overlying psychomotor retardation from depression, cerebellar ataxia or dyspraxia.

## Tremor
The Parkinsonian tremor is classicaly a resting *pill-rolling* low frequency (4-6 Hz) tremor that disappears with action. It generally starts unilaterally and progresses to involve both sides, but it should always affect one side more than the other. The tremor is exacerbated by other tasks like spelling or counting. It most commonly involves the upper limbs starting with a finger, but it can also involve the legs, chin, or jaw. Tremor is not always present in Parkinson disease, with about 75% of patients with Parkinson disease ever developing a tremor during their disease course.

## Rigidity
Rigidity can be the initial presentation of Parkinson disease manifesting with pain or stiffness, most commonly in the shoulder; often times it can be misdiagnosed initially as frozen shoulder or a rheumatological condition. The rigidity should be an increase in muscle tone that is not velocity dependent or directionally dependent (seen in both flexion and extension); sometimes it's called *lead pipe rigidity* but it may not be that apparent initially in the disease. The most sensitive place to look for rigidity is in the wrist. When the patient is in a relaxed position (hold the weight of the patient's arm in your hand so its completely relaxed), rigidity can be felt best with the reinforcement maneuver: patient is asked to perform an action on the contralateral side (raise contralateral arm up and down while looking for rigidity with a wrist circle maneuver, for example, on the ipsilateral wrist). Sometimes you can feel a tremor within the rigidity, which is referred to as *cog wheel rigidity*.

## Nonmotor Features
Patients may develop various nonmotor features of the disease that usually precede the motor symptoms. These include depression, anosmia, constipation, and REM sleep behavior disorder. Subtle cognitive deficits, involving memory and attention, may occur. Anxiety, apathy, and psychosis (glimpse things in corner of eye that are artificial or even hallucinations and delusions with a lack of insight). Autonomic dysfunction may manifest with urinary frequency or urgency, constipatoin, orthostatic hypotension, erectile dysfunction, diaphoresis, and drooling.
