## Focal and General Seizures
Generalized seizures involve both hemispheres *at onset*, while focal seizures involve only one region of the brain *at onset*, although they may progress to involve both hemispheres later. Usually general seizures are caused by genetic predispositions and manifest in children, whereas focal seizures tend to be due to a local pathology, such as a tumor, gliosis, or an abscess.

To distinguish seizures from syncope read *Syncope*.

## History
- Description of the episode
- Inciting events
- Any other neurologic symptoms
- Head trauma
- CNS infection
- Prior seizures
- Birth complications
- Age of onset
- Frequency of seizures
- Diurnal variation of events
- Presence of postictal confusion and its duration
- Cyanosis, usually present in tonic-clonic seizures
- Pallor, usually absent in tonic clonic seizures
- Breathing, usually labored in tonic clonic seizures

Differentiating autonomic seizures (Panayiotopoulos syndrome) from syncope can be challenging. Also, infants may not emonstrate typical feature sof seizures due to incomplete neurodevelopment (lack of CNS myelination leads to more migratory jerking rather than synchronous jerking see in tonic-clonic seizures in adults)

## Exam
- Perinatal trauma, suggested by asymmetries of physical development
- Cranial bruits, suggesting an AV malformation
- Papilledema, suggesting space occupying lesion or increased intracranial pressure
- Focal motor, sensory or reflex signs, suggesting space occupying lesion or increased intracranial pressure
- Abnormal skin pigmentation, suggesting neurodegenerative disorders\=
- Focal motor weakness (Todd paralysis), more common post seizure than post syncope
- Reflex asymmetry, more common post seizure than post syncope
- Babinski sign, more common post seizure than post syncope

## Types of Seizures

### Absence
- Demographic: 5-15 years with family history
- Description: abrupt, brief episode of decreased awareness wihout any aura or postictal symptoms; simple absence seizures have only an alteration of consciousness while complex absence seizures may have minor motor automatisms, which are most commonly eyelid fluttering - loss of tone, increase in tone, minor clonic movements of the facial musculature or extremities, and autonomic phenomenon like flushing, pallor, micturition, tachycardia, piloerection, and mydriasis may occur in complex, though rare; during the episode, the patient is immobile, normal breathing, no change in skin color, posture is maintained, and no motor manifestations (besides eyelid fluttering or minor automatisms) occur; usually lasts 10-15 seconds.
- After seizure: the patient immeidately resumes previous activities and may be unaware of the attack

A lot of the time, you can induce an absence seizure by having the patient hyperventilate for 3-4 minutes, which may facilitate a diagnosis.

### Tonic-Clonic Seizures
- Demographic:
- Description: with primary generalized seizures, usually no waning or aura occurs, but some prior myoclonic jerks may occur; during the tonic phase, sustained muscle contraction lasting 10-20 seconds occurs, followed by a clonic phase characterized by rhythmic jerking. Autonomic changes are common, including hypertension, tachycardia, apnea, mydriasis, piloerection, cyanosis, diaphoresis, and urinary or fecal incontinence. Injury from a fall or tongue biting are common. The postictal period is long, with consciousness taking a while to return. Brisk reflexes may be present

Sometimes, only tonic or only clonic seizures may occur.

### Complex Partial Seizures
- Demopgrahic
- Description: usually, the patient experiences an aura or warning symptom, followed by motor, visual, sensory, or psychic symptoms, including hallucinations, aphasia, or depersonalization, depending on the area involved; it usually lasts 1-2 minutes; partial seizures can progress to become tonic-clonic seizures; automatisms in complex partial seizures are more noticeable, and commonly include chewing, lip smacking, fumbling with objects, trying to stand, or walking; usually there is a postictal state with gradual return to normal consciousness.

### Comparison of Absence and Complex Partial Seizures

| Feature                         | Absence Seizure                    | Complex Partial Seizure                     |
| ------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Neurological status             | Normal                             | May have positive history or examination    |
| Age at onset                    | Childhood or adolescence           | Any age                                     |
| Aura or warning                 | No                                 | Common                                      |
| Onset                           | Abrupt                             | Gradual                                     |
| Duration                        | Seconds                            | Typically 1–2 minutes                       |
| Automatisms                     | Simple                             | More complex                                |
| Provocation by hyperventilation | Common                             | Uncommon                                    |
| Termination                     | Abrupt                             | Gradual                                     |
| Postictal phase                 | No                                 | Confusion, fatigue                          |
| Frequency                       | Possibly multiple seizures per day | Occasional                                  |
| Electroencephalogram            | 3 Hz generalized spike-and-wave    | Focal epileptic discharges or focal slowing |
| Neuroimaging                    | Usually normal findings            | May demonstrate focal lesions               |

## Diagnosing Seizures
### Essential
- CBC
- Urinalysis
- Drug screening
- Glucose
- Serum electrolytes
- MRI >> CT

### Additional
- Lumbar puncture
- Screening for amino acid disorders (in kids especially)
- EEG (epilepsy is a clinical diagnosis, so EEG cannot confirm the diagnosis on its own - normal findings do not exclude epilepsy)

## Psychogenic Nonepileptic Episodes
These are paroxysmal episodes of altered behavior that resemble epileptic seizures but lack the expected EEG changes
