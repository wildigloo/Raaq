#Surgical Site Infections [1-3]
##Classification of Surgical Site Infections
Deep incisional SSIs and organ/deep space SSIs must occur within 30 days of the operation if no implant is left in place or within 1 year after the operation is an implant is left in place. Superficial incisional SSIs must occur within 30 days post operation regardless of implant status.

###Superficial Incisional SSIs
Most common type of SSI. Confined to skin and subcutaneous tissues around an incision. Can be diagnosed when one or more of the following criteria are met:

1. Purulent discharge from surgical site
2. Organism isolated from surgical site
3. Clinical diagnosis of a surgical site infection by the surgeon
4. Intentional incision of the by a surgeon accompanied by at least one associated symptom such as swelling, erythema, pain, or warmth

###Deep Incisional SSIs
Extend deeper into tissues beneath the incision, affecting the muscles and fascia around the incision. Diagnosis requires at least one of the following conditions:

1. Purulent drainage from the deep incision but not from an organ/space
2. Deep incision spontaneously dehisces or is opened by a surgeon when the patient has at least one of the following: fever, localized pain, or tenderness
3. Abscess or other sign of deep incision infection found during reoperation, by histopathology, or radiology.

###Organ or Deep Space SSIs
Least common but most severe type of SSI. Infections extend beyond the skin, muscle, and surrounding tissues, affecting organs or spaces between organs. Often diagnosed by the drainage of pus from a drain placed in a body space. Diagnosis requires one of the following at least:

1. Purulent drainage from a drain placed into the organ space
2. Organisms obtained on culture from the involved space
3. Abscess or other sign of organ/space infection found during reoperation, by histopathology, or radiology.

##Bacterial Pathogens Usually Responsible for SSIs

###By Time
- Acute SSI (< 1 week postoperation): S. pyogenes, C. perfringens
- Subacute SSI (< 1 month and > 1 week postoperation): *S. aureus, S. epidermidis, Pseudomonas, E. coli, E. faecalis*

###By Procedure

1. Placement of grafts, prostheses, or implants: *S. aureus, S. epidermidis*
2. Cardiac: *S. aureus, S. epidermidis*
3. Neurosurgery: *S. aureus, S. epidermidis*
4. Breast: *S. aureus, S. epidermidis*
5. Ophthalmic: *S. aureus, S. epidermidis, Streptococci, gram negative bacilli*
6. Orthopedic: *S. aureus, S. epidermidis*
7. Noncardiac thoracic: *S. aureus, S. epidermidis, S. pneumoniae, gram negative bacilli*
8. Vascular: *S. aureus, S. epidermidis*
9. GI (Appendectomy, biliary, colorectal, gastroduodenal): *Gram negative bacilli, anaerobes; gastrodoudenal only: also streptococci and oropharyngeal anaerobes*
10. Head and neck: *S. aureus, streptococci; oropharyngeal anaerobes*
11. Obstetrics and gynecology: *Gram negative bacilli, enterococci, group B streptococci, anaerobes*
12. Urologic: *Gram negative bacilli*

##Preventing SSIs
###Preoperative Measures
- Optimizing patient health: smoking cessations, weight management, glycemic control, addressing preexisting infections (especially urinary tract or skin infections), and medication review (avoiding steroids)
- Preoperative antiseptic use (chlorhexidine)
- Hair removal
- Nasal decolonization: mainly in cardiac or orthopedic surgeries - screen for nasal MRSA colonization and treat with intransal mupirocin
- Oral hygiene and dental cleaning
- Antibiotic prophylaxis

##Wound Classification
- Class I/Clean: An uninfected operative wound in which no inflammation is encountered and the respiratory, alimentary, genital,
or uninfected urinary tract is not entered. In addition, clean wounds are primarily closed and, if necessary, drained with closed
drainage. Operative incisional wounds that follow nonpenetrating (blunt) trauma should be included in this category if they meet the
criteria.
- Class II/Clean-Contaminated: An operative wound in which the respiratory, alimentary, genital, or urinary tracts are entered under
controlled conditions and without unusual contamination. Specifically, operations involving the biliary tract, appendix, vagina, and
oropharynx are included in this category, provided no evidence of infection or major break in technique is encountered.
- Class III/Contaminated: Open, fresh, accidental wounds. In addition, operations with major breaks in sterile technique (eg, open
cardiac massage) or gross spillage from the gastrointestinal tract, and incisions in which acute, nonpurulent inflammation is
encountered are included in this category.
- Class IV/Dirty-Infected: Old traumatic wounds with retained devitalized tissue and those that involve existing clinical infection or
perforated viscera. This definition suggests that the organisms causing postoperative infection were present in the operative field
before the operation.
